Governance Essentials: Live Session Guide

For the instructor and the CLEAR facilitator. How to prepare for, run and close out the 2.5-hour virtual live session that completes the Governance Essentials program.

1. About the Session

The live session is where participants apply what they learned in the six self-paced modules, with peers from other boards and jurisdictions.

ItemDetails
Format2.5 hours on Zoom, with three rounds of breakout rooms
ParticipantsNew and experienced regulatory board members, both public and professional members, from many jurisdictions and professions. Don't assume a particular mix; a cohort may be all public members or all professional members.
What participants have completedModules 1–6 and the Communication Styles and Emotional Intelligence surveys. Each module ends with a reflection that participants were asked to bring.
MaterialsThe slide deck (with speaker notes on every slide), this guide, the participant guide (which includes the case) and the session evaluation link
Session goals
  • Compare how boards in different jurisdictions protect the public.
  • Apply the modules to a new case that crosses all six topics.
  • Practice what to say at the board table in difficult moments, adapting for colleagues with different communication styles.
  • Answer the questions participants bring from their own boards.
Design principles The session favors discussion and peer problem-solving over lecture or role play. Nothing depends on work outside the session beyond the module reflections, and breakout rooms are random, so no activity relies on a particular mix of participants.

2. Roles

The instructor leads the learning. The facilitator runs the technology so the instructor never has to.

Instructor

  • Leads every segment and debrief, using the speaker notes
  • Chooses which sticking points to cover, based on the poll
  • Visits breakout rooms where useful
  • Answers questions, and refers jurisdiction-specific legal questions to the participant's own counsel
  • Keeps the session to time, using the contingencies in Section 9

CLEAR Facilitator

  • Opens the Zoom room, manages admissions and records the session
  • Opens and closes breakout rooms, and broadcasts time warnings
  • Launches the poll and shares the results
  • Pastes the prepared chat messages in Section 11
  • Watches the chat and the virtual Q&A board, and passes questions to the instructor
  • Handles any participant technical problems

3. Before the Session

One to two weeks before

  • Instructor Complete or review all six modules and both surveys, so you know exactly what participants have seen.
  • Instructor Read this guide and the speaker notes, and rehearse the case debrief using the model answers in Section 7.
  • Facilitator Send participants a reminder with the session link, the participant guide, and a request to bring their Playbook pages and survey reports.
  • Facilitator Optional: invite participants to submit a question in advance. Add these to the virtual Q&A board before the session so others can upvote them.

The day before

  • Facilitator Complete the Zoom setup in Section 4, including the poll, and set up the virtual Q&A board.
  • Facilitator Fill in the bracketed placeholders on the cover and closing slides: date, instructor name, portal link, contact list deadline and recording end date.

Fifteen minutes before

  • Facilitator Open the room and admit the instructor.
  • InstructorFacilitator Run a quick tech check together: screen sharing, presenter view and a test breakout room.
  • Instructor Load the deck in presenter view.
  • Facilitator Paste the welcome message in chat as participants arrive.

How to use the poll results

The segment is 15 minutes: about 2 for the poll, 3 for a quick tour of all five topics, and 10 for the deep dives.

  1. Tour all five. While the results are on screen, give each topic its one-sentence headline (in the speaker notes on slide 8), so every participant hears all five.
  2. Dig into the top two. Spend about 5 minutes on each, using its slide and the "Ask the room" question.
If the results show…Then…
A clear top twoDig into those two.
A tie for second placeBreak the tie with a 10-second reaction vote ("thumbs up for this one, heart for that one"), or make the call yourself if time is short.
Three topics tied at the topCover all three at about 3 minutes each, using only the "Ask the room" question on each slide.
A clear winner and a scattered restSpend about 6 minutes on the winner and 4 on the runner-up.

Close the segment by noting that every topic comes up again in the case clinic, and that participants can post questions about any of them to the virtual Q&A board.

Activity notes: Public protection in practice

Every regulator can recite "protecting the public from harm," so the activity asks for the part that varies: where public protection gets hard in practice. In breakouts of three, participants:

  1. Name the harm their board deals with most often, and who it affects.
  2. Describe a time protecting the public pulled against something else, such as the profession's wishes, access to services, cost or workforce shortages.
  3. Agree on the competing pressure that came up most, to post in the chat in a word or phrase.

In the debrief, read several pressures aloud and group them. The point to land: every board shares the purpose, and the real work of a board member is weighing it against everything that competes with it. That is the thread through the rest of the session.

6. Sticking Points Reference

The concepts board members most often misunderstand. Cover the two or three the poll selects.

TopicKey pointsWatch for
One person, one role in a case
Module 5		A board member takes part in no more than one of investigating, prosecuting or deciding a case. Contact about a pending case from anyone outside the process is declined, referred to staff and reported to counsel."Our board is small; we have no choice." Ask what alternatives exist, such as alternate panel members or an independent tribunal.
One voice
Module 2		Disagreement belongs before the vote. Afterward, members explain and support the decision, and use the board's process to revisit it. One voice rules out campaigning against a decision; it doesn't necessarily mean hiding a vote that is public record.

Respect and humility matter here too. A board decision reflects the considered judgment of colleagues who heard the same evidence. Being on the losing side of a vote may mean the others saw something you didn't. Supporting the decision is a sign of respect for them and for the process, not a surrender of your values. If a decision genuinely conflicts with your values, raise it through the board's process, not outside it.		Confusing one voice with silence. Members don't have to pretend they agreed.

Treating a lost vote as a personal defeat. Ask the room: "When you've been outvoted, how did you come to terms with it? Did the board turn out to be right?" Invite participants to share how they process a decision they argued against.
When in doubt, declare
Module 2		Know the policy; declare early; seek advice privately; leave the room for the discussion and the vote; make sure the minutes record it. An undeclared conflict can remove the protection that normally covers good-faith decisions."Everyone knows everyone in our profession." That is exactly why declaring matters.
Rules clarify, not change, the law
Module 4		Rulemaking authority is delegated by statute. "Shall" makes it mandatory; "may" leaves it to the board. A rule can't change the statute or create new law. The board oversees; staff and counsel draft.Different governance models. Some boards make their own rules; in others, an umbrella or central agency does. Invite participants to describe how rules are made in their setting, and treat every model as equally valid. Avoid any suggestion that one model is better.
Participation is not competence
Module 6		CE hours show participation. Stronger programs combine self-assessment, learning that is assessed, and reviews of actual practice, and the board evaluates outcomes, not completion rates.Defensiveness about an existing program. Frame it as a question for the board to ask.

7. The Case and Model Answers

The case is set at the Westbrook Professional Standards Board, the fictional board participants followed through all six modules. It is deliberately compound: a good answer draws on every module.

Part A: The complaint

A patient complains about Jordan Ellis, a dental hygienist at a new low-cost clinic on Harbor Street. She says an unlicensed "clinical assistant" performed a deep cleaning that left her with an infection while Ellis was in another room. The clinic advertises prices well below other practices in the area.

At the board meeting, professional member Grace Liu says: "These cut-rate clinics are a menace. We should shut them down." Grace co-owns a dental practice two blocks away.

Q1. What is the risk to the public here, and what is not the board's concern?

A strong answer includes

  • The risk: a patient was harmed, possibly by an unlicensed person performing a procedure that requires a license, with inadequate supervision. Other patients at the clinic may face the same risk.
  • Not the board's concern: the clinic's low prices or its competition with other practices. Acting on those would be protecting the profession's market, not the public (Module 1, including the North Carolina dental board case).
Q2. Grace's comment and her practice: what should happen, and who should raise it?

A strong answer includes

  • Grace has a financial conflict: her practice competes with the clinic. She should declare it and step aside from any board decision about these clinics, with her departure minuted (Module 2).
  • Ideally someone raises it with Grace or the Chair privately first, so she can declare it herself. If it isn't addressed, it must be raised at the table.
  • Her call to "shut them down" also frames the issue as competition. The board's tools are the complaints process and, if needed, rules, not closing businesses it disapproves of.
Q3. What kind of concern is the complaint against Ellis? Who decides what, and in what order?

A strong answer includes

  • Mainly a conduct concern for Ellis (allowing an unlicensed person to perform a restricted task, a supervision failure), possibly with competence elements. The unlicensed assistant raises a separate scope-of-practice issue, and the clinic's owner may also bear responsibility.
  • The complaint goes through the normal process: intake, assessment (including whether any interim measure is needed), investigation, review and resolution (Module 5).
  • Board members shouldn't discuss the specifics at a public meeting. Anyone who might later decide the case must avoid forming a view now, and members involved at one stage don't take part in another.
Q4. Draft it: the exact words you would use at the table in response to Grace.

A model response

"Grace, I share your concern for patient safety. Before we go further, I think it would be right to note your practice's interest, so no one can question the board's decision later. The complaint itself should go through our normal process, so whoever decides it comes to it fresh. What this board can look at is whether our supervision rule is clear enough to protect patients."

Look for: respect for the colleague, the conflict named without accusation, the complaint kept out of the board discussion, and a turn back to public protection.

Part B: A gap in the rules

The investigation finds Ellis could see the treatment room. The clinic's lawyer argues that meets the rule requiring "direct supervision" of assistants, a term the rule never defines. Three other clinics use the same model. Ellis has completed every continuing education requirement.

A local reporter calls board members asking whether the board is "protecting patients or protecting dentists."

Q1. Is this a case for a new rule, a clarification, or something else? What must the board confirm first?

A strong answer includes

  • An undefined term in a rule that several clinics interpret differently is a classic case for an interpretive rule or an amendment defining "direct supervision" (Module 4).
  • First: define the problem and objective, confirm the board's authority with counsel, and consider options and their costs. Then follow notice and comment.
  • A new definition can't be applied retroactively to Ellis. Her complaint is decided under the rule as it stood. A staff memo can't serve as an enforceable rule in the meantime.
Q2. What does Ellis's CE record tell you, and what does it not?

A strong answer includes

  • It shows participation, not competence. It says nothing about whether she supervises safely in practice (Module 6).
  • If competence is in question, tools that look at actual practice would tell the board more. Strong participants may connect this to whether the board's continuing requirements are working.
Q3. What can the board say to the reporter, and who should say it?

A strong answer includes

  • The board speaks with one voice through its designated spokesperson, usually the Chair or executive director (Module 2).
  • Individual members don't comment on a pending complaint. Doing so could compromise the process and the confidentiality of the matter.
  • The spokesperson can explain the board's public protection role and that complaints are handled through a fair process.
Q4. Draft it: two sentences you would say if the reporter called you.

A model response

"Thank you for calling. I can't comment on any specific matter, but the board's role is to protect patients, and I'd refer you to our executive director, who speaks for the board."

8. Practice the Words

Groups agree on what they would say in three situations, then adapt their wording for a colleague with a different communication style. Use these model responses in the debrief if groups get stuck.

How participants use their own results

  1. Opening (slide 21): Review the four styles. Then invite participants, if they wish, to share their dominant style with a reaction or in the chat, so the room can see its own mix. A mix of styles is typical; point out that the board table looks the same.
  2. In breakouts (slide 22): When a group adapts its wording, members can draw on their own style and EQ results: "As a Contemplator, I'd want to hear the evidence first," or "My report says I tend to avoid conflict, so this is the one I'd struggle with."
  3. Debrief (slide 23): The third card asks what a participant's report showed about them in the practice. Take volunteers only.

Situation 1: The pressure

Grace repeats her call to shut the clinics down, and two members nod.

"I understand the concern. Our role is to protect patients, not to judge anyone's business model. If these clinics are putting patients at risk, let's look at the evidence, and at whether our supervision rule needs to be clearer."

Situation 2: The lost vote

The board starts rulemaking. You argued for waiting. A licensee asks what you think.

"The board decided to begin rulemaking so the supervision standard is clear for everyone. Licensees will have a chance to comment, and I'd encourage you to take it."

Look for: explaining and supporting the decision, without campaigning against it or revealing the debate (Module 2). In the debrief, ask how it feels to say this after arguing the other way, and whether the board might have been right.

Situation 3: The phone call

A colleague emails before the meeting: "Can I count on your vote?"

"I appreciate you reaching out. I'll decide after hearing the full discussion at the meeting, and I'd encourage you to make your case there so the whole board hears it."

Sharing results is always optional Participants share only what they choose from their Communication Styles and EQ reports, at the opening, in breakouts or in the debrief. Never ask anyone to disclose their results.

9. Facilitation Tips

Common situations

SituationWhat to do
A participant asks what the law requires in their jurisdictionAnswer at the level of principle, then refer them to their board's counsel. Don't give legal advice about a specific jurisdiction.
A participant starts describing a real, identifiable caseInterrupt gently: "Let's keep names and details out, but tell us about the principle you're wrestling with."
One voice dominatesThank them, then invite others by name or role: "I'd like to hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet."
A breakout group goes quietVisit the room and pose the first question directly, or ask each person for one sentence.
Some participants didn't complete the modulesDon't single them out. The activities work from experience; give them a moment to write a reflection on the spot.
A participant disputes a point from the modulesWelcome it as discussion. Practices vary by jurisdiction; ask the room how their boards handle it, then clarify the principle.
Technical problemsThe facilitator handles them privately so the session keeps moving.

Timing contingencies

If you are…Then…
Running longCover two sticking points instead of three. Shorten the Part A report-back to one answer. Trim Questions for the table to 15 minutes. Protect the break and the close.
Running shortCover a third sticking point, extend the case debrief, or give Questions for the table more time.

10. After the Session

  • Facilitator Within one business day, send all participants a thank-you that:
    • reminds them to complete the session evaluation in the learning portal;
    • says when and where the recording will be available, that only members of the cohort can see it, and that it will be removed when the cohort's access window closes, two weeks after the live session;
    • reminds them that their Playbook pages and survey reports are theirs to keep.
  • Facilitator Post the recording where only the cohort can see it, and remove it when the access window closes.
  • Facilitator Compile the cohort contact list from the portal opt-ins only, and send it to those who opted in.
  • Instructor Submit your teaching hours for payment in the Instructor Portal, along with any feedback about the program. Your evaluation report will be available in the portal shortly after the cohort's access window closes, currently two weeks after the live session.
